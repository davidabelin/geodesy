azdist_filter.py: The Sieve
Think of azdist_filter.py as a simple sieve.
Its Task: It examines one object at a time—a single line between two points.
Its Question: "Is the length of this one line extremely close to any of the 20+ numbers on my target list?"
The Math: For any given line, the check is a simple one-dimensional comparison: |distance - target| ≤ 0.0002. The chances of any single line hitting a target are low, but you are running ~20,000 independent checks, and a match on any one of them is a success.
With your very tight tolerances (0.0002), you are essentially asking for a near-perfect match on a single measurement. Out of ~20,000 pairs, it's statistically reasonable that you'd find 49 instances where a single line's length is extraordinarily close to one of your target values.
azdist_triangles.py: The Combination Lock
azdist_triangles.py is not a sieve; it's a combination lock.
Its Task: It examines a much more complex object—a set of three lines that must connect to form a triangle.
Its Question: "Do the proportions of these three connected lines simultaneously match the specific, interdependent proportions of a 3-4-5 triangle?" (Or a 30-60-90, etc.)
The Math: For a single triangle to match, it's not enough for one side to be correct. All three side lengths must align in a perfect ratio relative to each other. A tiny error in the measurement of just one of the three sides will throw the entire shape's proportions out of alignment.
This is a compound probability problem. The odds of three separate, random measurements all aligning to satisfy a strict, multi-part condition are astronomically lower than the odds of a single measurement hitting a target.
Why the Tolerances Must Be So Different
Imagine you are building a 3-4-5 triangle. In azdist_filter, you are just looking for individual planks of wood that are 3ft, 4ft, or 5ft long. You'll find a few.
In azdist_triangles, you are looking for a situation where three people (your points) happen to be standing at the exact vertices of a 3-4-5 triangle. For this to happen, the distance between P1-P2, P2-P3, AND P1-P3 must all be correct in relation to each other.
Because any small error in the underlying point data gets magnified when you calculate the angles and ratios, the tolerance for the final shape has to be much wider.
The tight tolerance in azdist_filter works because the condition for success is simple and one-dimensional.
The loose tolerance in azdist_triangles is required because the condition for success is complex and multi-dimensional. You have to allow for a greater margin of error on the final shape, otherwise you would never find a match unless your input points were already placed with superhuman precision.
